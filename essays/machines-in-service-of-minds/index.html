Essay · Building AI Around People

Machines in Service of Minds

Building AI Around People, Not the Reverse. Move the pointer, or touch the field: the structure bends around you.

Reuben ASK · 12 November 2026

1. The fork

In 1955, McCarthy, Minsky, Rochester and Shannon proposed that "every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it," and hoped to find how machines could "solve kinds of problems now reserved for humans" (McCarthy et al., 1955). Five years later, Licklider described a different arrangement. Humans would set the objectives, propose the hypotheses and judge the results, while machines would "do the routinizable work that must be done to prepare the way for insights and decisions" (Licklider, 1960). Steve Jobs later gave that second idea its best-known slogan, a bicycle for the mind.

Seventy years on, we have not chosen. The public conversation about AI moves between the two registers, sometimes promising to do the work people do and sometimes promising to expand what people can do. That ambiguity is tolerable for a builder. It is not tolerable for an organization, because the choice reaches it anyway, through hiring plans, workflow design and what it decides to measure. Brynjolfsson (2022) calls the pull toward replacement the Turing Trap: automation shifts bargaining power toward those who own the technology, while augmentation keeps people essential to the value being made, and the incentives of technologists, businesses and tax systems all lean toward automation. If the builders will not settle the question, the people who deploy their systems have to settle it themselves.

It is also more than an organizational question. It is a design question. Is the machine built around the person, or is the person expected to rebuild themselves around the machine?

2. What the default is costing

The first cost is at the entrance. Using ADP payroll data, Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen (2026) find that employment of workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations stands 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with less-exposed peers, with no comparable gap for experienced workers. The authors describe this as a descriptive indicator, not a causal estimate, and that caution should travel with the number. Anthropic's own account of its hiring points the same way: junior hires fell from 27% to 9% of technical hires since early 2025, and the company estimates that AI agent hours outnumber staff hours six to one, with staff hours assumed at forty a week (Wilmers et al., 2026).

The second cost is reversal. In an SHL survey, 19.1% of U.S. respondents who said they had been replaced by AI were later asked to return. Among those asked back, 51% wanted a significant pay raise and 42% wanted written guarantees about job security (Fortune, 2026). The third is spending that overlooks people. Deloitte's chief technology officer has said that companies put about 93% of their AI budgets into technology and only 7% into the people expected to use it, an estimate he offered from experience and not from a study (Fortune, 2025).

These are not proof that replacement caused the trouble. Two are vendor or executive figures and one is a working paper. Together they describe a pattern worth taking seriously, and the pattern has a plausible mechanism. Judgment forms by doing early-career work and getting feedback on it. If AI absorbs the routine work that juniors once did, the apprenticeship goes with it, and the supply of senior judgment shrinks a few years later.

3. Why "in the loop" is not enough

The usual answer to these worries is to keep a human in the loop. Human factors research has long warned that this is not a role but a leftover. Bainbridge (1983) pointed out the ironies of automation: operators are asked to monitor systems that rarely fail, which people cannot do well for long; their hands-on skill fades without practice; and when the system does fail, they must take over in the unusual situation that demands the most skill, with the least current picture of what is happening. Parasuraman and Manzey (2010) later showed how complacency and automation bias grow out of the same attentional limits.

The same pattern appears with today's tools. In a survey of 319 knowledge workers, higher confidence in generative AI was associated with less critical thinking, though the study does not establish causation (Lee et al., 2025). In a field experiment with 758 consultants, AI improved speed and quality on tasks inside its capability, but on a task outside it, consultants using AI were 19 percentage points less likely to produce a correct answer (Dell'Acqua et al., 2023). The frontier is jagged, and a person who cannot see where it lies will trust the tool in the wrong place.

The alternative is not less automation. Shneiderman (2020) argues that high automation and high human control can coexist, and that designing for both raises performance while supporting mastery and responsibility. That is what it means for a human to be in the lead by design, and not only in the loop on exceptions.

4. Building AI around people

This is the premise of User Behavior Architecture applied to AI: the system changes, and the person does not have to. Leading is not the same as adapting. The person sets direction, and the system carries the burden of fitting how that person actually works. Three design principles follow. They are proposals to be tested, not findings.

First, sense the person, not only the task. A system that assumes a rested, vigilant and expert user at all times is built around an imagined average. It should respond to the person's state, workload and experience, and ask for attention when it matters instead of all the time. Second, protect skill instead of absorbing it. Where work builds judgment, keep part of that work with the person, make the system's reasoning easy to inspect, and make verification cheap. The evidence on critical thinking and skill fade says this is where the damage accumulates. Third, keep agency visible. The person should set intent and boundaries, see what the system did, and see where its competence ends, which is exactly the information the jagged frontier hides.

There is also something worth protecting. Loaiza and Rigobon describe five groups of human capability, Empathy, Presence, Opinion, Creativity and Hope (EPOCH), and find that tasks requiring them are less susceptible to automation and strong candidates for augmentation. A system built around people treats these as the part to strengthen, not the part left over.

5. What an organization can do now

None of this requires a new model. Decide, at the level of strategy, that the aim is to amplify human judgment, and measure it: the share of early-career roles, the share of work that still forms opinions under real constraints, internal mobility, and how often automation is later reversed. Treat a worsening trend as a design failure, not as the price of progress. Redesign roles so that agents handle execution, retrieval and first drafts, while people set intent, define boundaries, decide where values or context matter, and own the outcome on novel or high-stakes calls. And fund the human side in proportion to the technical side, with real budget for role redesign, skill protection and trust, because a spending split like 93 to 7 is a plan for the problems described above.

6. The choice

Seventy years after Dartmouth, the question is no longer whether machines can simulate people. It is whether we will build them to serve the people who live and work beside them. The machine should be built around the mind, and the mind should not have to be rebuilt around the machine.
